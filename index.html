See You Tomorrow

Some connections are built not through grand gestures or unforgettable events, but through the quiet rhythm of ordinary days. A shared meal, a familiar cup of tea, an easy conversation that lasts longer than expected — small moments that gradually become something worth holding onto.

“See You Tomorrow” reflects on a bond between two people living in different worlds, with miles between them and lives of their own to return to. There is no need to define what they have or ask it to become something else. The beauty lies in the time they share, the happiness they wish for each other, and the simple comfort of knowing they will meet again. And when those everyday moments eventually become memories, gratitude can be a gentler farewell than sadness.

Sometimes, being part of someone else's journey, even for a while, is more than enough.

Maybe There Still Are

Memory has a way of keeping the smallest details while letting the bigger picture fade. A missed stop, a passing remark, a drink someone recommended without much thought at the time. We may forget most of an experience, only to find that a few fragments remain — and that they somehow mean more to us later than they did when they happened.

“Maybe There Still Are” follows those fragments through two place names and a glass of sangria. It isn't an attempt to reconstruct a day or explain what it all meant. Instead, it lingers in the space between remembering and understanding, where familiar details begin to connect in ways they never did before.

Perhaps the pieces were always there. Perhaps time simply gave us a reason to look again.

Still Wearing It

Sometimes, the most meaningful gifts are the ones that quietly become part of everyday life. What begins as someone else's idea gradually turns into a habit of your own — not through sudden transformation, but through small reminders that make it a little harder to ignore what you already know.

“Still Wearing It” is about that imperfect process. There are excuses, creative arguments, occasional victories, and plenty of evidence that doesn't quite support the case being made. Progress comes slowly, and not every number tells a flattering story. But somewhere between the data and the daily routine, something starts to change.

It's also a song about the person behind the gift: someone whose encouragement stays present even across a few thousand miles. The goal isn't perfection. It's simply to keep showing up, keep learning, and keep trying — one day at a time.

The Silent Observer

For six weeks, a borrowed home develops its own quiet rhythm. With the owner away, two cats keep watch from different corners of the house: one visible in the window, the other just beyond the narrator's line of sight. Their routines continue, whether anyone is watching or not.

“The Silent Observer” is a song about the quiet patterns we notice when we spend enough time in one place. Nothing dramatic happens, and nothing needs to be explained. One presence is seen; the other is simply known to be there. Together, they become part of a temporary world that feels familiar for a while, then belongs to someone else again.

Some stays leave stories to tell. Others leave only the memory of having briefly fit into a life that was never quite our own.

My Hand Will Still Be There

During six weeks of caring for two cats in a friend's home, the narrator discovers that trust looks different in each of them. Bao approaches readily, while Lee-Kway keeps her distance, sometimes accepting an offered hand and sometimes choosing to walk away. Neither response can be demanded or hurried.

“My Hand Will Still Be There” is about learning to respect the pace at which trust develops. The hand remains offered, without expectation or disappointment becoming a reason to withdraw it. There's no guarantee that tomorrow will bring a change, and no way to know which small moment might matter.

Sometimes, caring for someone means knowing when to reach out—and being willing to leave the choice to them. The promise isn't that trust will come. It's that the hand will still be there.

We don't always remember the things that connect our lives. Sometimes, they remember us.

Into Thirds

We pick up habits from other people all the time, often without realizing it. A particular way of folding a towel, a small gesture repeated until it becomes second nature. Years later, we may find ourselves doing the same thing without remembering where it came from.

“Into Thirds” is a quiet reflection on those little pieces of other people that remain with us. There's no dramatic revelation, no clear memory to unlock — just an ordinary Sunday, a blue towel, and a familiar motion that suddenly feels worth questioning. The habit carries on even when its origin has disappeared.

Perhaps the things we inherit without noticing are the ones that settle most deeply into our lives. We may never remember who taught us, or when we began. The towel gets folded anyway.

Printed in Black, Turning to Gray

An old receipt turns up in a jacket pocket, carrying the precise details of a purchase that has almost entirely vanished from memory. The time, the price, the batteries — all recorded in black ink, even as the paper slowly fades. What remains is a small, ordinary transaction from a day that no longer feels familiar.

“Printed in Black, Turning to Gray” reflects on the strange imbalance between what we remember and what the world records for us. A receipt can preserve the exact minute and cost of an errand, but it cannot tell us what we were thinking, what happened before, or why that particular moment mattered enough to leave a trace. The document survives, but the day itself is mostly gone.

There is something quietly moving about that. Even the most forgettable moments once occupied a real place in our lives. Sometimes all that remains is a fading piece of paper, holding on to details we never knew we would lose.

The Hidden World

What if an ordinary sidewalk held a secret that most people would walk right past? “The Hidden World” begins with that simple idea and follows the discovery of a world hiding in plain sight.

The song is inspired by geocaching, an outdoor treasure-hunting activity in which people use GPS coordinates to find containers hidden by others. Some are tucked into parks, built into everyday surroundings, or concealed in places that reveal themselves only to someone who knows where to look. Finders sign a logbook, return the container to its hiding place, and leave the discovery for the next person.

But the real discovery in this song is bigger than finding a hidden container. It's the moment when a familiar place becomes unfamiliar in the best possible way — when a small piece of paper connects you to strangers who have stood in exactly the same spot, and the world suddenly seems full of possibilities you never noticed before.

Sometimes, all it takes is looking a little closer.

How Many More?

Geocaching turns an ordinary trail into a string of small discoveries. Using GPS coordinates to search for hidden containers, you can spend an entire day exploring places you might otherwise pass without a second glance. And once you find one, it's surprisingly easy to start looking for the next.

“How Many More?” captures the momentum of a day spent searching by bike, with one person always ready to move on and the other asking the question that keeps the journey going. Thirty-two finds later, the day is over, the results are recorded, and the numbers have become part of a much larger story. Yet there's no single discovery that explains why it all mattered, and no obvious finish line.

Maybe that's the nature of the pursuit. The places keep coming, the numbers keep growing, and somewhere along the way, the search itself becomes the point. Long after the bikes are home, one question remains: how many more?

First of the Month

By now, the puzzles have become something to look forward to. On the first day of each month, a new one appears, and a familiar online thread begins to fill with guesses, references, connections, and ideas. The narrator is eager to join in, but the others often seem to be several steps ahead. He reads, thinks, types something out, and sometimes has to leave it in the box.

This song captures the experience of being part of a group before quite feeling like you belong. The puzzle gets solved, plans begin for Saturday, and the narrator assumes everyone else will go without him. Then someone asks the question that changes the day: “How about you, Miles?”

The final image is deliberately ordinary: a crowded van pulling away for a day out. He hasn't solved the puzzle, and he hasn't suddenly become an expert. But he's been invited along, and that turns out to be enough.

Sometimes, the real discovery isn't the answer on the screen. It's finding yourself included in what happens next.
